About This Campaign

This isn't a complaint. It's a fix.

Ghent Streets is a community-led effort to amend Norfolk's HC-G2 zoning district to permit neighborhood-scale retail by right. The tools to do this already exist inside the city's own code. We're just asking the city to use them.

Who We Are

Rooted in Ghent. Working through the process.

This campaign was started by a resident of 801 Colonial Avenue — the heart of the HC-G2 district. He is a former board member of the Ghent Historic District, which means he knows the process, knows the players, and knows the neighborhood well enough to know that the problem isn't the architecture. The problem is the zoning code.

This is not a fight against historic preservation. Ghent's residential fabric is worth protecting — that's not in dispute. What is in dispute is whether protecting the Victorian streetscape requires banning the corner coffee shop, the neighborhood bookstore, the wine bar, and the bodega. The answer, self-evidently, is no.

The text amendment we're proposing would permit small-scale, ground-floor retail uses by right on parcels with street frontage — the same model Norfolk has already deployed successfully on Colley Avenue and 21st Street through its Pedestrian Commercial Overlay district. This is not an experiment. It's an application of what Norfolk already knows how to do.

The Neighborhood

HC-G2: extraordinary houses, dead sidewalks.

The HC-G2 district is one of the most architecturally intact nineteenth-century residential neighborhoods in Virginia. Its grid is dense and walkable by design — platted in 1893 as Norfolk's first planned community. The bones are exactly what urban planners spend decades trying to recreate.

And yet the sidewalks are dead. At 7pm on a weekday, Colonial Avenue feels like a suburban cul-de-sac with better cornices. The retail uses that naturally colonize the ground floors of dense urban neighborhoods — café, dry cleaner, wine shop, florist — cannot legally open here without a Conditional Use Permit. A CUP means $5,000, six months, a public hearing, and City Council approval. Most potential operators don't bother.

The result is predictable: two nonconforming commercial uses (the 7-Eleven at the south end and the Harris Teeter at the north), surrounded by dead frontage, broken stoops, and concentrated unhoused presence in the vacancies. It doesn't have to be this way.

The Argument

Three things are true at once.

Historic preservation is legitimate. HC-G2's standards — ARB review, design guidelines, setback requirements — exist for good reason and should stay. We are not asking to demolish anything, add a parking garage, or build a strip mall.

Active ground floors are compatible with historic character. The neighborhoods that inspired HC-G2's design guidelines — Georgetown, the Fan District in Richmond, the Garden District in New Orleans — all have thriving pedestrian commercial life. Their streets are alive precisely because they never banned the corner store.

The CUP process is a structural barrier, not a filter. A conditional use permit doesn't sort good uses from bad ones — it sorts well-funded uses from neighborhood-scale ones. The result is that the only retail that survives the process is corporate retail with lawyers. That's backwards.

"Performance standards, not case-by-case approvals. That's the difference between a neighborhood that works and one that doesn't." Campaign position paper, Policy Brief No. 1

The Process

How a text amendment works in Norfolk.

A zoning text amendment in Norfolk goes through three stages: a recommendation from the Architectural Review Board, a public hearing before the Planning Commission, and a final vote by City Council. The process is open to any property owner or resident in the affected district.

The contact for initiating a text amendment application is Jeremy Sharp in the Planning Department: (757) 439-4833 or jeremy.sharp@norfolk.gov. The relevant ordinance sections are Table 3.6.11 (HC district uses), Table 3.9.21 (PCO uses), and the Norfolk Zoning Procedures Manual Section 3.3(B).

The relevant planning document is plaNorfolk2030, specifically the Land Use Strategies chapter, which already supports mixed-use development in pedestrian-scale neighborhoods. We are not asking Norfolk to change its values — we're asking it to act on them.

What This Campaign Is Asking For

  • Amend Table 3.6.11 to permit neighborhood-scale retail uses by right on ground floors of parcels with street frontage in HC-G2
  • Use performance standards — hours, square footage, signage — to manage compatibility without CUP requirements
  • Clarify that ARB review applies to physical alterations, not change-of-use alone, consistent with Virginia Code § 15.2-2306
  • Model the amendment on Norfolk's existing PCO language, which is already proven and defensible
  • Establish this as precedent for other HC sub-districts facing the same structural problem
