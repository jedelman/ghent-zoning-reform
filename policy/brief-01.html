Policy Brief · No. 1

The Case for By-Right Retail in HC-G2

A proposed zoning text amendment to permit neighborhood-scale retail uses on ground floors in Norfolk's Ghent Historic and Cultural Conservation district — and the planning, legal, and economic argument for doing it.

Norfolk, Virginia HC-G2 District Zoning Ordinance § 3.6 / Table 3.6.11 plaNorfolk2030 — Land Use Strategies Version 1.0 · 2025

Executive Summary

Norfolk's HC-G2 zoning district covers most of the Ghent neighborhood — one of the city's most densely populated, most walkable, and most historically significant residential areas. Yet the zoning code currently prohibits all street-facing retail, restaurant, and personal service uses from operating on ground floors without a Conditional Use Permit. This brief argues that this prohibition is a policy failure, documents its consequences, proposes specific text amendment language to Table 3.6.11 of the Norfolk Zoning Ordinance, and shows that the proposal is consistent with plaNorfolk2030 and with precedents the City of Norfolk has already established in its Pedestrian Commercial Overlay districts.

I. What HC-G2 Currently Allows

Norfolk's Historic and Cultural Conservation districts are governed by Article 3, Section 3.6 of the Zoning Ordinance, with permitted uses listed in Table 3.6.11. Under the current table, HC-G2 permits the following as principal uses by right (P): single-family and limited multi-family residential uses, institutional uses such as schools and churches, and home occupations subject to performance standards.

The following uses are currently prohibited or require a Conditional Use Permit (C) in HC-G2. A blank cell in the use table is not a default or an ambiguity — the table key states explicitly: "BLANK CELL = PROHIBITED." (Norfolk Zoning Ordinance, Table 3.6.11 key.)

  • Eating and drinking establishments of any kind
  • Retail sales, general
  • Personal services (barbers, salons, tailors, etc.)
  • Office uses in ground-floor street-facing positions
  • Neighborhood-serving commercial uses of any kind

The practical consequence: any entrepreneur who wants to open a coffee shop, corner store, bookshop, or dry cleaner on Colonial Avenue or any other HC-G2 street must file a Conditional Use Permit application, pay a $1,080 application fee, retain legal and planning consultants (typically $2,000–$8,000 in professional fees), wait approximately six months, appear at a public hearing before the City Planning Commission, and receive approval from City Council. The ARB must also weigh in on any exterior modifications. Almost none do. The streets stay dead.

"The process is the prohibition. The CUP requirement does not regulate retail — it eliminates it."

II. Why This Matters: The Cost of Dead Streets

The effects of commercial prohibition in a dense residential district are well-documented in urban planning literature and visible on the ground in Ghent. They include:

Loss of informal surveillance. Jane Jacobs identified "eyes on the street" as the primary mechanism of urban safety. Commercial frontage generates foot traffic at all hours; residential-only blocks do not. The concentration of unhoused individuals on HC-G2 streets is partly a function of the absence of active commercial uses that would generate natural foot traffic and informal community presence.

Suppressed property values and tax revenue. Mixed-use ground floors in walkable residential neighborhoods command measurable premiums in assessed value and generate direct sales tax and BPOL revenue that pure residential uses do not. A conservative estimate for HC-G2: activating 20 ground-floor retail units averaging 1,000 sq ft at $30/sq ft gross annual revenue would generate approximately $600,000/year in commercial activity, yielding roughly $35,000–$50,000/year in city tax revenue from a currently inert land use.

Loss of neighborhood-serving amenities. Residents of HC-G2 must drive or travel significant distances for goods and services that, in a properly-zoned walkable neighborhood, would be within a five-minute walk. This increases VMT, reduces social cohesion, and undermines the quality of life that makes Ghent a desirable neighborhood in the first place.

Park degradation. Ghent's parks are underused not because they are poor parks but because the surrounding streetscape does not generate the foot traffic that makes parks feel safe and activated. Retail on adjacent blocks creates natural park activation.

III. The Precedent: PCO Districts Work

Norfolk has already demonstrated that it knows how to create mixed residential-commercial streets through its Pedestrian Commercial Overlay (PCO) districts. Colley Avenue, 21st Street, 35th Street, and Riverview all carry PCO designations that permit ground-floor retail and eating/drinking uses by right within residential base zoning. These corridors are, by any measure, the most commercially vital streets in the city outside the downtown core.

HC-G2 has the same density, the same walkability, and the same demand as these corridors. It lacks only the zoning permission. The amendment proposed here would bring HC-G2 into alignment with what the city has already proven works, without requiring a new overlay district, a rezoning, or any change to the residential character protections that HC-G2 also provides.

IV. The ARB Question

Any proposal to amend zoning requirements in historic districts requires a recommendation from the Architectural Review Board before it proceeds to the City Planning Commission. This is appropriate — the ARB exists to protect the physical character of the district, and that function should be preserved.

This proposal is designed to work with ARB authority, not around it:

  • The amendment permits uses by right. It does not permit physical alterations by right.
  • Any exterior modification associated with a new commercial use — signage, facade changes, new windows — still requires a Certificate of Appropriateness from the ARB.
  • The ARB's design review authority is unchanged. What changes is the use table: a café in an existing ground-floor space would not require a CUP, though it would still require a zoning certificate and, for any exterior work, ARB review.

This framing should be central to the ARB presentation. The amendment expands use rights without expanding physical modification rights.

V. Proposed Text Amendment Language

The following is proposed amendment language for Table 3.6.11: Principal, Accessory, and Temporary Use Table for Historic and Cultural Conservation Base Zoning Districts.

For each use category listed below, we propose changing the HC-G2 column from blank (prohibited) or C (conditional) to P (permitted by right), subject to the performance standards in Section VI below.

TABLE 3.6.11 PROPOSED AMENDMENTS — HC-G2 COLUMN USE CATEGORY CURRENT PROPOSED ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── COMMERCIAL USES — RETAIL & SERVICE Retail sales, neighborhood-serving [blank] P (grocery, pharmacy, hardware, books, gifts, clothing — no single use to exceed 3,500 sq ft ground floor GFA) Eating and drinking establishment, [blank] P small-scale (no drive-through; no more than 50 seats; no live entertainment without CUP) Personal services [blank] P (barber, salon, tailor, dry cleaner, copy center, similar) Professional services, ground-floor [blank] P (financial, legal, medical, dental — ground floor only; street-facing window display required) Art gallery / studio [blank] P Fitness studio, small-scale [blank] P (yoga, martial arts, dance — no more than 3,000 sq ft) NOTES: • All uses above are PRINCIPAL uses permitted BY RIGHT (P) on parcels with ground-floor street frontage in HC-G2. • All uses are subject to performance standards in Section 4.3.3.X (new section — see Section VI below). • Exterior alterations to structures remain subject to Certificate of Appropriateness (ARB review). • Drive-through facilities remain prohibited in HC-G2. • Large-format retail (>3,500 sq ft GFA) remains C (CUP). • Bars/taverns (primary alcohol sales) remain C (CUP). • Adult uses remain prohibited.

VI. Proposed Performance Standards (New Section 4.3.3.X)

The following performance standards shall apply to all neighborhood-commercial uses permitted by right under the amended Table 3.6.11 in HC-G2:

  1. Ground floor only. Commercial uses are permitted on ground floors only. Upper floors of any structure shall remain residential or institutional.
  2. Street-facing activation. A minimum of 50% of the ground-floor street-facing facade shall consist of transparent glazing. Display windows or interior activity visible from the sidewalk are required.
  3. Size limits. No single retail or eating/drinking establishment shall exceed 3,500 square feet of ground-floor gross floor area without approval of a Conditional Use Permit.
  4. Hours. Standard hours of operation are 7:00 a.m. to 11:00 p.m. Extended hours (to 2:00 a.m.) require a CUP for uses within 100 feet of a residential use.
  5. Signage. Signage shall comply with Section 5.7 of the Zoning Ordinance and, for any exterior signage, shall require a Certificate of Appropriateness from the ARB.
  6. Outdoor seating. Outdoor seating on private property is permitted. Outdoor seating extending into the public right-of-way requires a separate encroachment permit.
  7. Parking. No minimum off-street parking requirement for uses under 2,000 sq ft GFA. Uses over 2,000 sq ft: one space per 500 sq ft. Bicycle parking: two spaces minimum per establishment.
  8. Deliveries. Commercial deliveries shall not block travel lanes and shall be completed between 7:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m.

VII. Consistency with plaNorfolk2030

The City of Norfolk's comprehensive plan, plaNorfolk2030, includes several provisions directly supportive of this amendment. The following are verbatim from the adopted plan:

  • Outcome LU1.2 (Land Use Strategies chapter, p. 2-18): "Development regulations and practices that support a quality built and natural environment." This is the plan consistency standard cited in staff reports for prior HC-district text amendments approved by the Planning Commission.
  • Action LU1.2.5: "Revise zoning regulations, developing new commercial categories and appropriate regulations that better reflect the current function of commercial areas as neighborhood-serving, community-serving, or region-serving." Permitting neighborhood-serving uses by right in HC-G2 is precisely this action.
  • Action N3.1.6 (Neighborhoods chapter): "Evaluate the effectiveness of Pedestrian Commercial Overlay (PCO) zoning districts, and consider potential ordinance revisions to ensure that they serve the purpose for which they were intended." The plan's own comment notes: PCO districts "encourage the location of specialty retail, entertainment, and restaurant uses in concentrations and to a scale that encourages pedestrian movement." This amendment extends that model to HC-G2.
  • Action N5.1.2(b): "Revise regulations to require that retail and commercial uses have strong ground-floor visual and access connections to the sidewalk, with walkability and sense of place as a priority."
  • Action N5.1.12 (Greater Ghent): The plan calls for exploring a Business Improvement District for Ghent (N5.1.12(b)) and utilizing historic district design guidelines to ensure appropriately designed development (N5.1.12(d)) — both consistent with an activated, commercially viable ground-floor environment.

The plan consistency finding here is not a stretch. The plan names PCO districts as the model for pedestrian-scale commercial activation, calls for neighborhood-serving commercial regulations, and has specific Ghent actions supporting economic vitality. This proposal implements all three.

VIII. Process and Timeline

A zoning text amendment to Table 3.6.11 proceeds as follows under Norfolk's Zoning Ordinance:

  1. Initiation. Text amendments may be initiated by City Council, the Planning Commission, or application by a property owner or citizen. A neighborhood advocacy organization or individual property owner in HC-G2 may file the application. Fee: $915 for a zoning text amendment (Norfolk Planning Fee Schedule, effective July 1, 2024).
  2. ARB referral. Because the amendment modifies requirements in a historic district, the ARB must provide a recommendation before the Planning Commission public hearing. The ARB meets monthly; allow 30–45 days for scheduling.
  3. Planning Commission public hearing. Staff prepares a report; public notice is published in The Virginian-Pilot two weeks prior. The commission votes to recommend approval or denial to City Council.
  4. City Council vote. Council holds a final public hearing and votes. Approval requires a simple majority.

Estimated total timeline: 4–8 months from application to Council vote, assuming no significant opposition delays scheduling.

IX. Anticipated Objections and Responses

"This will change the residential character of the neighborhood." The amendment permits commercial uses on ground floors only, with size caps, signage controls, and all ARB exterior review retained. The physical character of the district — which the HC designation exists to protect — is unchanged. The use of a building can change without any exterior modification whatsoever.

"There isn't demand." The displacement argument is visible on the ground: Colley Avenue, Granby Street, and the HC-G1 corridor absorb the commercial demand that HC-G2 cannot legally serve. Residents of HC-G2 patronize those businesses — the demand exists and is expressed elsewhere. More directly: two existing commercial uses operating in the district (the 7-Eleven and Harris Teeter) demonstrate that HC-G2 residents generate commercial activity at sufficient density to sustain retail. The constraint is supply, not demand.

"The CUP process exists for a reason." CUPs are appropriate for uses that require case-by-case evaluation of impacts that cannot be adequately managed by performance standards. Neighborhood-scale retail in a dense urban residential district does not meet this bar — the impacts are well understood, the precedent is extensive (every PCO corridor in Norfolk), and the performance standards proposed here are more protective than what the PCO districts require.

"This is better handled as a PCO overlay." A PCO overlay on specific corridors is a valid alternative approach. The difference is scope: a PCO would apply only to designated streets; this amendment applies HC-G2-wide, treating the entire district as the walkable urban neighborhood it is rather than carving out selected corridors for different treatment. The PCO approach could be pursued in parallel as a fallback.

"If there's demand, operators will use the CUP process — the barrier isn't that high." This is the strongest version of the opposing argument, and it deserves a direct response. First, the absence of commercial activity is itself revealed preference: operators are not using the CUP process at meaningful scale, even though the relevant market (dense, high-income urban residential) demonstrably supports retail. The CUP fee and legal costs are not prohibitive for a well-capitalized corporate operator — and indeed, the two uses that have survived on Colonial Avenue (7-Eleven, Harris Teeter) are precisely that. What the CUP process filters out is the independent operator who cannot absorb six months of legal costs and timeline uncertainty before signing a lease. The result is not a neutral market test — it is a capital screen. Second, the absence of named operators in this brief reflects the current state of the campaign, not the absence of demand. Identifying specific operators who have considered HC-G2 and declined is a priority task; that evidence, when gathered, will convert a structural argument into a direct testimonial one. Third, the counter-argument proves too much: if the CUP process is not a meaningful barrier, then removing it for neighborhood-scale retail costs the city nothing in terms of planning control, while the upside — if demand exists — is the commercial activation this brief documents. The asymmetry favors the amendment.

X. Recommendation

We call on the City of Norfolk Department of City Planning to initiate a text amendment to Table 3.6.11 of the Zoning Ordinance consistent with the language proposed in Section V of this brief. We further request that the Architectural Review Board for the Ghent Historic District be presented with this proposal at its next available meeting and be invited to provide a recommendation to the Planning Commission.

This is a modest, well-precedented, city-plan-consistent amendment. It will not transform Ghent overnight. But it will remove the regulatory barrier that currently prevents the neighborhood from being what its residents, its density, and its architecture naturally want it to be: a place where people walk, gather, and stay.
